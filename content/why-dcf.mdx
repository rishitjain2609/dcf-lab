## What a DCF is

Picture a lemonade stand that's going to run for the next ten summers. To find out what that
stand is worth *today*, you wouldn't just add up all the lemonade money it will ever make. A
rupee ten summers from now isn't worth the same as a rupee in your hand right now, because money
today can be invested and grow, while money ten years away carries the risk it might never show
up at all. So you estimate how much cash the stand will throw off each year, then "discount"
each year's cash back to today's value using a rate that reflects how risky the stand actually
is. Add all those discounted years together, and that sum is what the stand is worth today.

A company is the same idea at a much bigger scale. Forecast the cash it will generate, discount
each year back to today, add it up. That's a discounted cash flow model, start to finish. The
rest of this site is just the mechanics of doing that carefully.

## Where it's used

Analysts, bankers, and buyers all use the same math for different jobs.

<UsesGrid />

Equity research uses a DCF to publish an implied value, the way this site's own
[models](/models) do. M&A buyers run one to sanity-check a price against what the target can
actually generate. PE firms underwrite a deal against a hold period and an assumed exit, running
the machinery in reverse. And in an IPO, bankers triangulate a price range partly off a DCF,
alongside comparable companies.
