## What a DCF is

Picture a lemonade stand that will run for the next ten summers. To work out what it's worth
*today*, you can't add up all the lemonade money it will ever make. A rupee ten summers from now
is worth less than a rupee in your hand. You could invest today's rupee and watch it grow, and the
rupee ten years away might never show up. So you estimate the cash the stand will bring in each
year, then "discount" each year's cash back to today using a rate that matches how risky the stand
is. Add up the discounted years and you have the stand's value today.

A company works the same way, at a bigger scale. Forecast the cash it will generate, discount each
year back to today, and add it up. The rest of this site covers how to do each of those steps
carefully.

## Where it's used

Analysts, bankers and buyers run the same math for different jobs.

<UsesGrid />

Equity research analysts use a DCF to publish an implied value, the way this site's
[models](/models) do. In M&A, a buyer runs one to check an asking price against the cash the target
can produce. Private equity firms work backwards from a holding period and a planned exit price. In
an IPO, bankers set the price range partly from a DCF and partly from comparable companies.

## Where it breaks

<LimitsBand />

The [terminal value](/guide/terminal-value) step explains where that 70% comes from, and
[sensitivity tables](/guide/sensitivity-tables) show the 2-point swing. A DCF still earns its place.
Its answer can only be as good as its assumptions, so each [model](/models) on this site lists every
assumption and its source, and you can check them yourself.
