## What a DCF is

Picture a lemonade stand that's going to run for the next ten summers. To find out what that
stand is worth *today*, you wouldn't just add up all the lemonade money it will ever make — a
rupee ten summers from now isn't worth the same as a rupee in your hand right now, because money
today can be invested and grow, while money ten years away carries the risk it might never show
up at all. So you estimate how much cash the stand will throw off each year, then "discount"
each year's cash back to today's value using a rate that reflects how risky the stand actually
is. Add all those discounted years together, and that sum is what the stand is worth today.

A company is the same idea at a much bigger scale: forecast the cash it will generate, discount
each year back to today, add it up. That's a discounted cash flow model, start to finish — the
rest of this site is just the mechanics of doing that carefully.

## Where it's used

- **Equity research** — analysts build a DCF to publish an implied value and justify a rating on
  a stock, the way this site's own [models](/models) do.
- **M&A** — when one company buys another, the buyer runs a DCF to sanity-check the price against
  what the target can actually generate, so they don't overpay based on vibes.
- **Private equity** — a PE firm underwrites a deal against a specific hold period (often 3–7
  years) and an assumed exit, essentially running the whole DCF machinery in reverse to solve for
  "what return do we get if we buy at this price and sell at that one."
- **IPO pricing** — when a company goes public, bankers and the company itself triangulate a
  price range partly off a DCF, alongside comparing it to similar already-listed companies.

## Where it breaks

The honest limits, because a tool this widely used deserves them stated plainly:

- **Most of the value is a guess about forever.** As the [terminal value](/guide/terminal-value)
  step on this site shows, roughly 70% of a typical DCF's value often comes from one assumption
  about growth *after* the forecast period ends — not from the carefully-built years in between.
- **Small changes move the answer a lot.** The [sensitivity tables](/guide/sensitivity-tables)
  step shows a 2-percentage-point change in the discount rate moving the implied value by nearly
  30%, with zero change to any actual business assumption.
- **It can be reverse-engineered.** Because there are so many assumptions to turn, a DCF can be
  built to justify almost any price if the assumptions get picked *after* deciding what answer
  you want, instead of before.

None of that makes a DCF useless — it makes it a tool whose answer is only as good as its
assumptions, which is exactly why this site tracks every published implied value against what
actually happened afterward, on each [model's page](/models), rather than publishing a number and
moving on.
