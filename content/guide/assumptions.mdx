---
title: Assumptions
description: Turning historicals into a forecast, the step most DCFs get wrong.
---

This is the step where most DCFs quietly go wrong, because it's the easiest one to fudge. Every
other step in this guide is just arithmetic, plug in a number, get an answer. This step is a
forecast, a claim about the future, dressed up as a spreadsheet cell.

The honest way to do it is backwards from how it feels natural to do it. Don't start from "what
assumption gives me the answer I expect." Start from "what does this market actually look like
in five years, and does this company's share of it grow, shrink, or hold steady." A food-delivery
company growing 40% a year today almost certainly isn't growing 40% a year in year five, because
the market itself runs out of new customers to add. A mature utility company isn't going to
suddenly start growing 15% a year either, unless something concrete changes (new plant, new
regulation, new market). Each assumption should have a one-sentence reason behind it that isn't
"because it makes the valuation come out to roughly what I expected."

## The running example's assumptions

Carried over from [Setup & conventions](/guide/setup-and-conventions), five years, flat.

| Year | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Revenue growth | 10% | 10% | 10% | 10% | 10% |
| EBITDA margin | 20% | 20% | 20% | 20% | 20% |
| D&A, % of revenue | 5% | 5% | 5% | 5% | 5% |
| Capex, % of revenue | 6% | 6% | 6% | 6% | 6% |
| Δ NWC, % of revenue increase | 10% | 10% | 10% | 10% | 10% |
| Tax rate | 25% | 25% | 25% | 25% | 25% |

Flat across all five years on purpose, to keep the arithmetic easy to follow by hand. A real
model almost always fades growth down and may flex margins. See
[Checks](/guide/checks) for why a flat-forever growth rate is also exactly the thing to
interrogate in the terminal value.
