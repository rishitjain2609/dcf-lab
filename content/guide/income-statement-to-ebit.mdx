---
title: Income statement to EBIT
description: From revenue to EBITDA to EBIT.
---

EBITDA is what the business earned from running its core operations, before touching anything to
do with how it's financed (interest), taxed, or the accounting wear-and-tear on its assets
(depreciation and amortization, "D&A"). It's a useful quick-comparison number, but it's not real
profit — a factory's machinery really does wear out every year, even though no cash leaves the
bank account on the day a machine depreciates.

EBIT puts that wear-and-tear back into the picture by subtracting D&A. Here's the part that trips
people up: a few steps from now, D&A gets *added back* when building free cash flow, since it
never cost any actual cash. So why subtract it here just to add it back later? Because taxes are
calculated on EBIT, not EBITDA — the tax office lets a company deduct depreciation before taxing
it, so D&A has to come out here to get the tax bill right, even though the cash itself never left.

## Worked example

| Year | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Revenue (₹ cr) | 1,100.00 | 1,210.00 | 1,331.00 | 1,464.10 | 1,610.51 |
| EBITDA (20% margin) | 220.00 | 242.00 | 266.20 | 292.82 | 322.10 |
| D&A (5% of revenue) | 55.00 | 60.50 | 66.55 | 73.21 | 80.53 |
| EBIT | 165.00 | 181.50 | 199.65 | 219.62 | 241.58 |

```
ebitda[t] = revenue[t] × 20%
ebit[t]   = ebitda[t] - da[t]
```
